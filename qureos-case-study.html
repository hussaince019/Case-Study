Platform case study

The AI operating system for hiring. And the month I found out we'd built it in the wrong shape.

Seven AI capabilities for recruiting, one platform, and a structural mistake I had to undo: we shipped it as a funnel, and recruiters don't work in funnels. This is the story of the rebuild. I'm the only PM on it.

Role
Product Manager and Designer. Sole PM.
Company
Qureos, a $5M-funded AI recruiting startup
Owned
Strategy, discovery, PRDs, design, prototype, sprints, delivery
Since
July 2024
01 — Problem

The bottleneck was never finding candidates.

A recruiter can pull a hundred plausible candidates before lunch. What they can't do is talk to a hundred people. So the funnel narrows on a CV skim, and the first real conversation happens with a shortlist that was already chosen on the weakest evidence in the process.

Everything between those two points — chasing phone numbers, formatting notes, juggling timezones, remembering who said what — is coordination. It's most of the job, and none of it is the job.

The 94 candidates in the gap are decided on a CV skim, or never looked at. That gap is the product.
Interviewed
Skimmed or unseen

Sourcing volume scaled years ago. Assessment capacity never did. Every hiring tool built since has made the first number bigger.

 "Can't a recruiter just use ChatGPT?"

Yes, and they do. Recruiters already paste job descriptions into ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini to draft outreach, summarise a CV, or rewrite a role. It genuinely saves time, and I'd never argue otherwise — a general assistant is very good at the writing tasks inside hiring.

What it can't do is hold the process. It doesn't know who you contacted last Tuesday, it can't run a phone interview at 9pm in another timezone, it can't score fifty applicants against the same criteria consistently, and it forgets the candidate the moment the tab closes. There's no pipeline, no talent pool, no audit trail, and nothing trained specifically on how hiring decisions get made.

General AI helps with tasks inside hiring. It doesn't run hiring. The gap isn't intelligence, it's memory, structure and continuity across a process that takes weeks.

02 — What broke

We built a funnel. Recruiters don't work in funnels.

The first version of the platform encoded the obvious mental model: source, then screen, then interview, then decide. Each capability assumed the one before it had run. To interview someone you went through sourcing. To screen, you needed a job. It looked coherent on a whiteboard.

Then real usage arrived, and almost nobody entered at step one.

A recruiter with fifty CVs from a client wants to screen, and has no interest in sourcing. An agency with a shortlist already agreed wants to run interviews, nothing else. A hiring manager just wants a job posted. Someone else only wants the talent pool, kept clean and searchable, for roles that don't exist yet.

Every one of them hit a step they didn't need in order to reach the one they did. The drop-off wasn't in any single feature. It was in the order I'd imposed between them.

The architecture wasn't wrong. The sequencing was. I'd designed the happy path and made it the only path.

03 — The fix

Six front doors, one memory.

Each module became a complete product on its own, with its own entry, its own empty state and its own reason to exist without the others. That was the engineering half. The product half was making sure independence didn't cost coherence.

The answer was a shared context layer. Every module reads and writes the same candidate, the same criteria, the same history of what was asked and decided. Screen someone today and interview them in three months, and the interview knows what the screening found. Use only the talent pool for a year, and the day you post a job, your pool is already the candidate source.

Sourcing

Describe who you want in plain language and get a ranked set. Works with no job posted, no pipeline, nothing set up.

Usable alone

Job posting

Publish a role and collect inbound applicants. Useful on its own if a job board is all you needed.

Usable alone

AI screening

Bring fifty CVs and score them against weighted criteria. No sourcing run required, no job required.

Usable alone

AI interview

Upload a shortlist you already have and run interviews by link, phone or WhatsApp. Entry point, not a final stage.

Usable alone

Campaigns

Sequenced outreach to anyone in your pool, whether they came from sourcing or from your own list.

Usable alone

Talent pool

A large, sorted, searchable pool as a product in itself — organised for roles that don't exist yet. Also the spine everything else writes to.

Also the context layer

Pipeline management sits across all of them: one board, one candidate record, whichever door they came through.

04 — Defensibility

Owning the whole funnel is the only way to learn what happened next.

Most hiring tools see a slice, so they never learn the outcome. Handle only sourcing and you never find out who got hired. Handle only interviews and you never see who applied.

Because our modules share one context layer, we capture the thing sliced tools structurally cannot: what happened after the recommendation. Who advanced, who was hired, who lasted. That's the moat, and it's a consequence of the restructure rather than a feature we shipped.

Category mapMy framing, not a vendor claim

An ATS records the outcome but doesn't run the assessment. An AI screener runs the assessment but never sees the hire. A general assistant is strong on any single task and holds none of the process. Top right means the recommendation and the result land in the same system.

06 — Unblocking the company

Half of engineering's interruptions were CS asking for a database change.

We're a startup, which means the customer-facing systems a mature product automates don't exist yet. No self-serve payment flow, no automated credit top-up, no account-merge tool. So when a client paid by invoice, or ended up with two accounts, or ran out of credits, the only path was a message in the support channel and an engineer running something manually.

That cost twice. Engineering lost focus to a steady queue of small requests, and CS couldn't answer a customer without waiting on someone else. The team closest to the customer had the least ability to act.

Before scoping anything I had the load measured properly, against the production database and the support channel history. Ten weeks, 157 threads, 56 of them requests for engineering to touch production data. Just over a third of everything in the channel, roughly six a week — and half of those were a single request type.

The obvious answer was to build automated billing and account management. That was the wrong call for this stage. Months of work, dependent on payment infrastructure we don't have, solving a scale problem instead of the one blocking CS this week.

So I scoped an internal admin app instead: role-based access, one screen per real task, built on the backend APIs that already existed, frontend-first with mock data so every workflow could be validated with the teams who'd use it before a backend estimate was written. Team heads provision their own users.

The endpoints are shipped and tested. The TA tool is live in production. The remaining step is a production service credential for the rest, which is a rollout task rather than a product one — and the reason I can state the impact as a projection instead of a result.

07 — Product

What shipped, and the decision inside each screen.

I design this platform as well as manage it. Tap a marker to see the call behind it.

  1. 1

    One question, one input. The empty state asks something instead of presenting a dashboard. A first session has exactly one job, so everything else is deferred.

  2. 2

    Accelerants, not setup steps. Three optional shortcuts, none of which block the primary action. A checklist at the top of a first session is where activation dies.

  3. 3

    Flat nav — every module is a front door. This is the restructure made visible. One level deep, no module buried inside another's flow.

  4. 4

    Credits always visible. Usage is the pricing unit, so hiding it creates bill shock and support tickets.

VoiceRecruiters describe a role better out loud than in writing. Dictation removes the blank-page problem for the least technical user in the funnel.
ThemingTokenised components mean a full theme is a variable change, not a redesign. Dividend from centralising the design system.
  1. 1

    Three assessments, independent state each. The modular restructure at row level. A candidate can be interviewed without being screened, or screened without a sourcing run, so none of these share one status.

  2. 2

    Status is system-derived and never editable. Keeping it separate from Stage means the machine can't appear to have made a hiring call.

  3. 3

    Stage is the recruiter's column. Only a person moves it. Same board whichever module the candidate entered through.

  4. 4

    Weighted criteria, and when scoring runs. Must-have down to good-to-have, plus a plain note on why a score isn't there yet. No unexplained blanks.

  5. 5

    Fit score stays empty until evidence exists. A default number would read as a judgment nobody made.

08 — Growth & PLG

Modularity only pays off if strangers can reach it.

Once any module could be a first experience, the signup flow became the bottleneck. I work with the growth and data team on that path, against one rule: ask for nothing until the moment it's genuinely needed, and never ask twice for something we can fetch ourselves.

The friction ladderWhat we ask, and when
On arrival Nothing. Create a job with AI or run a sourcing search, no account. Prove value before asking for anything. The product is the demo.
To save work Email, then one click on a verification link. No password to invent, no OTP to copy, no reset path to maintain.
To post a job Phone, company LinkedIn, website. Fraud protection where it matters. Profile built from the website automatically, not typed.
Never A setup wizard, or a demo call before first value. Both trade activation for data we can get later, or fetch ourselves.

Each rung has to earn its place by protecting someone. The bottom rung is the one most products get wrong.

01

Value before signup. The product demos itself.

On the landing page a visitor can create a job with our AI and try the sourcing module without an account. They see real output first, then sign up to keep it. The account isn't the price of entry, it's the price of saving your work.

Why not a demo request

A demo form turns intent into a calendar invite and loses everyone who won't take a call. This works because of the restructure — sourcing alone is a complete experience, so there's something real to hand a stranger.

02

Passwordless signup. No password, no OTP.

Email in, verification link out, user inside the product. Nothing to invent, nothing to copy from another app, nothing to forget and reset later.

Ruled out

Password plus OTP, which felt more secure and was argued for. It adds two failure points for security a verification link already provides, plus a permanent support cost in resets.

03

Verification is progressive, not upfront.

A new user gets the whole platform on a verification link. We ask for identity at the one moment it protects someone: posting a public job. Then phone, company LinkedIn, and we pull the rest from their website automatically to build the profile for them.

Ruled out

A profile wizard at signup — cheapest data collection point, most expensive activation cost. And auto-rejecting a company whose domain doesn't match: mismatches route to human review, because a false rejection costs a real customer.

04

Instrumented with the data team, in Segment.

Events across the journey — landing, first result, signup, verification, first job, first screening, first interview — so we can see per step where users progress and where they stall, per module. That last part matters: with six front doors, an average activation rate hides which door is broken.

The rule

Tracking events ship inside the definition of done. A feature isn't finished until they fire, because the sprint where you go back for it never arrives.

09 — Decisions

Five calls, and what each one ruled out.

Every decision here closed off something a reasonable person would have built instead. The second column is the case study.

01

Independence over a guided path.

The call

Every module became usable alone, with its own entry and empty state, writing to a shared context layer. Six front doors instead of one.

Ruled out

Keeping the guided funnel and fixing drop-off with onboarding — tooltips, a progress checklist, a better wizard. That treats a structural problem as an education problem, and it's what most teams do because it's cheaper than a restructure. It would have taught users to tolerate a path they didn't want.

02

Shared context, not shared screens.

The call

The talent pool became the layer everything reads and writes. Upload candidates inside an interview and the pool is created and named automatically. New surfaces reuse parsing and applicant logic that already exists.

Ruled out

Full independence, where each module owned its own candidate object and upload path. That's faster per feature and produces six tools sharing a login — modular in the worst sense, with the same person entered three times under three IDs.

03

The AI ranks. A person decides.

The call

Assessments return a recommendation with reasoning beside it, not behind a click. Status is system-derived, Stage is only moved by a human.

Ruled out

Auto-rejection below a score threshold — the obvious efficiency win, and customers asked for it. A false negative silently costs someone a job and generates no feedback signal, so we'd never learn we were wrong. Company-verification mismatches route to human review for the same reason.

04

Completion over a flattering funnel.

The call

A candidate enters an interview only with a CV, a valid phone number and an email, enforced at upload. Invited and In Progress stay two distinct states.

Ruled out

Partial records chased later, which raises candidates added and lowers interviews completed, since delivery runs on link, phone and WhatsApp. And merging two states into one simpler label, which would have permanently hidden the largest drop-off in the product.

05

The product is the growth engine. The service is the wedge.

The call

Two motions, deliberately unequal. Self-serve is where compounding value lives. The managed service earns near-term cash, wins buyers who'll never adopt software, and feeds real hiring outcomes back as labelled data.

Ruled out

Treating them as equal bets and letting service revenue set the roadmap. That builds bespoke features for single clients and ends with a custom software shop wearing a product's name.

10 — What's next

Modular was step one. Open is step two.

Making the modules independent was the precondition for everything below. You can't let a customer compose their own flow, or call one capability from outside, while the capabilities are chained together. This is live work, not a wishlist.

In progress

Agentic sourcing — customers build their own agents

Rather than running our flow, a recruiter composes an agent: where to look, what to weigh, when to run, what to do with results. The product moves from a pipeline we designed to a pipeline they design. The hard question isn't capability, it's how much control you can hand over before the output stops being trustworthy.

In progress

ATS integrations

Most companies won't replace their ATS, and asking them to is how you lose the deal. Qureos sits on top of the stack they already run, reading and writing candidates rather than demanding migration. This is what turns "another tool" into a layer.

Next

MCP server — Qureos callable from anywhere

Exposing the platform over MCP so an assistant a recruiter already uses can source, screen or check pipeline without opening Qureos. A bet that the interface layer is moving, and the durable position is being the system underneath it rather than the window onto it.

12 — The hard questions

Questions founders ask me about this work.

The follow-ups this page invites, answered the way I answer them in the room.

The opinion moved, it didn't disappear. Inside a module we're extremely opinionated — screening criteria are weighted, interviews have a fixed structure, the pipeline has one candidate record. What we stopped being opinionated about is the order you use them in, because that's the one thing the customer knows better than us. Guidance still exists; it's just contextual rather than a gate.

Because the drop-off wasn't confusion, it was refusal. Users understood the path perfectly and didn't want it — an agency with a shortlist isn't confused about sourcing, they just don't need it. Onboarding fixes a comprehension problem. We had a fit problem, and better tooltips on a step someone doesn't need still leaves them on a step they don't need. That said, I'd have known this a month earlier with instrumentation in place, which is on me.

They do use them, and for drafting outreach or summarising a CV a general assistant is genuinely good. It isn't a competitor on tasks; it's a competitor on nothing, because hiring isn't a task. It's a process that runs for weeks across dozens of people. A chat window has no pipeline, no talent pool, no memory of who you contacted, can't phone a candidate in another timezone, and can't score fifty applicants against identical criteria consistently. General AI helps inside hiring. It doesn't hold hiring.

It conflates two things: proving control of an inbox, and adding steps. A verification link proves inbox control — which is what a password reset falls back on anyway, so a password is a weaker second factor pretending to be a first. An OTP proves the same thing again in a worse interface. The real risk isn't account access, it's fraudulent employers posting fake jobs, handled where it occurs: phone plus company LinkedIn at job post, mismatches to human review, never auto-rejected.

Some will take the output and leave, and that's acceptable. What they can take is one result; what they can't take is the pipeline, the pool, the interview history, the context. Value compounds only inside an account. The alternative is a signup wall in front of a product nobody has seen work. I'd rather pay in leaked first results than in unmeasured drop-off.

The bus factor is real and I treat it as a design constraint — which is exactly why the design system lives in code with Storybook rather than in my Figma, and why PRDs state non-goals explicitly. The honest limit is throughput: one person deciding, designing and prototyping caps how many bets run in parallel. The stage I'm built for is the one where that's an advantage — pre-scale, where iteration speed beats division of labour. Past it, my first hire is a designer, because the system survives me and a taste bottleneck doesn't.

Two things, and they're related. I designed the happy path and shipped it as the only path — capability arrived faster than any thinking about how someone would actually enter the product. And I had no instrumentation, so I learned it from support tickets and sales calls instead of from a funnel, which cost weeks. If I ran it again I'd define how a user enters and moves through the whole system before shipping the second module, not the fifth — and I'd have the events live before the first one.
